# Mechanical details: retention, couplings, seals, cables, stops, lids

Started 2026-11-13, as part of the push to specify the rover's own hardware (`docs/hardware-spec.md`). These are the items that `strut-spec.md`, `drivetrain-spec.md` and `body-redesign.md` list as "not modelled". Every choice here is **mine (recommended, owner has delegated this kind of call), from general engineering practice, not from a source**, and none is checked in CAD yet; sizes marked ASM are assumptions. Where the owner had already decided something, it is quoted.

## 1. Wheel to stub shaft

**Choice:** a D-flat on the 3 mm stub shaft and one M2 x 3 cup-point set screw through the wheel's apex collar onto the flat. The existing collar (`strut-spec.md`, apex boss and collar at |x| = 136.85) gets a tapped M2 radial hole. **Why:** a flat plus a set screw is the standard way to fix a hub to a 3 mm shaft; the wheel torque (at most 0.14 N.m at the wheel for the step case, `drivetrain-spec.md`) is well within what one M2 set screw on a flat holds (ASM, no figure checked). **Add:** a drop of thread-locker; the screw is the wheel's only axial retention, so the wheel also needs the bearing pair's inner faces to bear on a step (the existing 4 mm bearing span already does). **Change if:** the wheel is ever removed often (then a second screw, 90 degrees apart, on a second flat).

## 2. Spindle tube in the strut-tip bore

**Choice:** the 19 mm tube is a slip fit in the 19.1 mm bore, held by a **split clamp**: a slit through the tip's bore wall and one M2 screw across it, plus retaining compound in the bore as a backup. The tube's 1.5 mm outboard cap is its axial stop in one direction, the clamp holds the other. **Why:** `strut-spec.md` already calls for a clamp; a slit-and-screw clamp is the simplest, and the strut is also the motor's heat path, so a tight clamp helps. **Check done (2026-11-14, `hardware/strut/pivot_checks.py`, hand arithmetic with assumed material figures):** the tip wall round the 19.1 mm bore is 2.45 mm (24 mm tip), and the clamp is only the 3 mm plate thick. **Strength is not the problem:** the motor's stall reaction (0.31 N.m) needs a bore pressure of 0.4 to 0.9 MPa (a hoop stress in the tip wall of 1.4 to 3.6 MPa); the 6x bump load on the wheel (53 N, 41 mm from the clamp) puts a 2.2 N.m moment on the tube, which a 3 mm clamp resists with two edge forces of about 720 N, an edge pressure of about 38 MPa (6061-T6 yields at about 275 MPa, ASM); the tube itself sees 6.8 MPa in bending. **The weak point is stiffness:** over a 3 mm clamp length, a 0.1 mm slip fit tilts the tube by 1.9 degrees if it is not clamped. **So:** a tight fit (a few hundredths of a millimetre) plus retaining compound, not a slip fit and a screw; and **the M2 clamp screw needs an ear at the slit**, since a 2.45 mm wall cannot carry a screw head and thread (the plate is 3 mm thick, so an ear in the plate's plane would hold an M2 thread with about 1.5 diameters of engagement). **Done in CAD (2026-11-14, `hardware/strut/strut.py`; `COLLAR_IN`, `SLIT_W`, `SCREW_X`):** each strut's tip boss gets a **4 mm collar on its inboard side** (toward the body wall at |x| = 70; the collar's inboard face is at |x| = 73.25 for strut A and 77.25 for B, 3.25 mm clear of the wall for A), making the clamp **7 mm long** instead of 3; the spindle tube starts as far inboard (the motor stays where it was, so the extra 4 mm of tube is empty sleeve, and on strut B the collar surrounds the motor's last 4 mm); a **0.6 mm slit** runs from the bore through the tip's outer wall along the arm; and a **2.0 mm clamp-screw hole with a 3.9 mm counterbore crosses the slit** at 10.8 mm from the tip centre, at the middle of the plate and collar (7 mm of material, so the head and thread fit: the earlier 3 mm plate could not). **Effect (`pivot_checks.py`):** the bore edge force in a 6x bump falls from 724 N to 326 N (edge pressure 38 to 17 MPa) and the unclamped tilt from 1.9 to 0.8 degrees. **Checked:** `strut.py` finds interference between strut A and B only at the same 12 equal-angle pairs as before (the two struts pointing the same way, which the 60 degree rule excludes anyway), and 0.00 mm3 against the body, the hub, the wheels and the spindle at the default pose. **Not checked:** the collar against the body at every strut angle (only the default pose); the real clearance to the carbon body, whose side wall is at 70 mm, not Hammond's; and the retaining compound's cure space, since a bonded tight fit is the plan.
**If the wheel wobbles in the build:** a longer clamp would need a thicker tip, which strut B cannot take inboard (the 1 mm gap to strut A's plate) or outboard (the wheel's end plate); a bolted flange on the tube (three M2 screws on a 22 mm circle) is the alternative, with a larger tip boss (about 28 mm).

## 3. Motor-to-stub coupling

**Choice:** a single aluminium sleeve, 3 mm bore each end, 7.5 mm long (the length the CAD has room for, `drivetrain-spec.md` calls it tight), with one M2 set screw per end, the motor side onto the Pololu's D-flat and the stub side onto a flat. **Why:** it needs no part bought beyond a rod of aluminium, and the load is small. **Fallback if the screws do not fit in 7.5 mm:** bond the sleeve to the stub with retaining compound and use one set screw on the motor side only. **Note:** the Pololu #3068's output is a 3 mm D-shaft (`hardware-spec.md`, section 6), so the motor side is a standard D-flat.

## 4. Dust and seals

**Choice: shielded ball bearings (the "ZZ" type) and a labyrinth step, no rubber seals.** The wheel's collar overlaps the tube's cap by about 1 mm with a 0.2 mm gap (ASM), and the same at the hub housing. **Why:** a rubber lip seal adds friction that the 0.15 N.m wheel budget and the 0.17 N.m strut hold do not want, and the use case is an indoor tabletop (`docs/sim-spec.md`) where dust is not a function requirement. **Not covered:** fine dust that works through a labyrinth over hours; if the rover ever runs on regolith-like material, revisit with lip seals and a stronger servo.

## 5. Hub: adapters and pinions fixed to the servo horns

**Choice:** each horn adapter and pinion is a flanged part **bolted to the servo's supplied metal horn through the horn's own screw holes** (M2, ASM: the horn's pattern and thread are not confirmed), with the horn on the servo's 25-tooth output spline and its centre screw. **Why:** it uses the servo's own spline, so no shaft is broached and the servo output keeps its own backlash rating. **To do:** read the real horn dimensions from the Feetech drawing before the adapter diameters are fixed (the 20 mm horn adapter and the 28 mm housing flange in `hub.py` are placeholders); gear teeth at the 1:1 pair are still plain discs at the pitch circle.

## 6. Wire passage across the strut pivots

Each strut carries one wheel motor, so **two wires per strut, eight across the four pivots**, and the inner strut shaft (5 mm steel) is solid, so a wire cannot run through it.
**Choice:** a **limited-rotation strut with a service loop**: a flat or silicone-insulated two-conductor cable looped around the strut's hub boss, entering the body through a hole in the wall beside the hub's spot-face with a printed or moulded strain-relief and a dab of sealant. **Why:** slip rings at 19 mm are bulky and the wire count is only 8. **Corrected on 2026-11-14 (the first version said about plus or minus 170 degrees and claimed that the chassis simulation's poses stay within a half turn: that claim was not supported and was wrong):** the chassis simulation (`hardware/drivetrain/chassis_sim.py`) allows each strut any angle over the full circle, subject only to the two struts staying at least 60 degrees apart; the self-righting search (`hardware/strut/pivot_checks.py`, which runs it) moves strut A by up to **195 degrees** and strut B by **180 degrees** from the start stance in a single sequence. So the strut travel must be nearly a full turn: item 7 now finds that a stop leaving a dead sector of 10 degrees or so, placed at the angles given there (strut A between absolute 15 and 30 degrees, strut B between 345 and 360), strands none of the inverted rest states, so the travel is about 345 degrees and the cable wrap about 100 mm. **Cable numbers (my arithmetic, `pivot_checks.py`):** wrapping or unwrapping the cable on the 30 mm boss (cable centre at radius 16.5 mm) takes 98 mm of cable for 340 degrees of travel, 104 mm for 360, 156 mm for 540; the free run along the arm to the tip is 55 mm; five turns of a 1.3 mm pair fit along the boss's 7 mm, but the 1 mm gap to the neighbouring strut limits a pair to one layer. **Routing, worked through 2026-11-14 (my reasoning from `strut.py` and `hub.py`; not yet in CAD):** **strut A's wires** leave the motor tail (flush with A's plate inboard face, |x| = 77.25), run along the arm's inboard face (the plate is flush with the outer face of the boss, so a 4 mm channel lies between it and the body wall), and enter a **ring-shaped space of about 3 to 4 mm height around the boss (radius 15 to 19 mm), between the boss's inboard face at 73.25 and the wall at about 70**, where the slack is held as a spiral of about one turn (107 mm per turn at radius 17 mm; the 340 degree travel needs 98 mm) before a **2 mm hole through the wall at about 19 mm from the hub axis**, outside the 30 mm spot-face. **Strut B's wires cannot go that way:** B's inboard side faces strut A's boss and plate with only the 1 mm gap, and any route round A's boss crosses A's arm, which sweeps through 340 degrees, so A would pinch the wires. **So B's wires go through the strut B inner shaft:** make it hollow (5 mm outside, 3 mm bore; the bending stiffness stays 87% and the shear stress at the servo's 7.4 V stall torque, about 1.8 N.m, is about 84 MPa, against several hundred MPa for steel, my arithmetic), run the two wires from the tip through a groove in B's plate to the shaft's outboard end and in through a cross hole, and out of the shaft's inboard end in the body, where the twist over about 30 mm of free wire must stay within the 340 degree travel (a twisted silicone wire pair is routine at plus or minus 180 degrees over 30 mm, my judgement; it is the part to prove on a bench). **Done in CAD (2026-11-14):** `hardware/hub/hub.py` now makes the inner shaft hollow (`INNER_BORE` 3.0 mm, 4.0 g each) with a **1.2 mm cross hole at |x| = 52.25**, in the 1.5 mm window between servo B's horn adapter (ends at 51.5) and the hollow shaft and gear plane (starts at 53.0), so the wires leave the shaft there and pass out between the adapter flange and gear A (the hub's parts against each other: 0 overlapping pairs of 45); `hardware/body/block.py` cuts a **2 mm feedthrough hole through each side wall, 19 mm above the hub axis** (y = 0, z = 49, |x| 60 to 70; the block still re-imports as one solid and every placement is clear of it). **Cable CAD finished (2026-11-14, later; `strut.py`, `hub.py`, `hardware/strut/cable_check.py`):** (1) **a 1.5 mm notch through each collar's pivot side** (3.8 mm long, the collar's full 4 mm height) lets the wires out of the tube's open tail; (2) **strut B's plate has a 1.5 x 1.0 mm groove on its inboard face from the collar to the pivot, continuing across its boss to the bore**; (3) **the hollow shaft has a second cross hole (1.0 mm at |x| = 81.75) just behind that groove**, so strut B's wires enter the shaft's bore **inside B's boss, not at the shaft's outboard end as I first planned** (the shaft's outer 4 mm stays solid), run inboard inside the shaft and leave through the first cross hole (1.2 mm at |x| = 52.25); (4) strut A's wires need no groove: they run free in the 4 mm channel between its plate and the wall. **Checks:** the hub parts against each other 0 overlapping pairs of 45; the struts, motors, spindles, wheels, body and hub show the same interference as before (strut A against strut B only at the same 12 equal-angle pairs; 0.00 mm3 against the body at the default pose). **Wire lengths per wire (`cable_check.py`, 345 degrees of travel, wound half each way from mid-travel plus 10 degrees): strut A about 134 mm (43 along the arm, 54 wound round the boss's ring, 12 through the wall, 25 inside), strut B about 140 mm (56 along the arm and groove, 29.5 inside the shaft, 10 wound round the shaft, 21 to the wall hole, 25 inside).** **Two points to watch:** the wrap round the shaft (B) is at a 3.0 mm radius, which is 5.0 wire diameters for a 0.6 mm wire, exactly the usual minimum bend (my rule of thumb); **finer wire (32 AWG, about 0.45 mm) gives a margin, but carries less (about 0.5 A continuous against 0.9 A for 30 AWG, my estimate; the motors' stall of 1.5 A is a burst before the driver's limit)**; and the 1.5 mm window holds the two wires side by side in one layer (1.2 mm), which the 1.0 turns of wrap fit. **Still open:** the wires' route from the wall holes to the power board (inside the body, in the cavity design), the ring's exact clearance to the neighbouring strut's boss (1 mm gap; free space, not modelled), the bench proof of the twist, and a connector or solder pads where the wires meet the motor.

## 7. Strut stops

**Superseded on 2026-11-14 (later the same day): a one-step dead gap, placed right, strands nothing.** Two searches of the chassis simulation's state graph (`hardware/drivetrain/chassis_sim.py`: strut angles on a 15 degree grid, one strut moving at a time, at least 60 degrees between the struts, the body settling downhill, from every rest state to an upright rest) decided this:

1. `hardware/strut/stop_window_check.py` (windows about home, starts anywhere or near home): full-circle travel strands none of the 1,494 inverted rest states; **the 340 degree window I first recommended (from -145 to +195 about home, a 30 degree dead sector) strands 3 of 458 starts within 90 degrees of home, 51 of 810 within 120, and 280 of 1,494 anywhere**; the symmetric plus or minus 170 degrees strands about half; more travel (405, 450, 540 degrees) does not help, because the struts cannot pass each other.
2. `hardware/strut/stop_gap_search.py` (builds the physical graph once, then tries every placement of the dead gap for each strut, 24 x 24, for gaps of one and two grid steps): **a one-step (15 degree) gap strands no inverted rest state anywhere (0 of 1,494) for 59 of the 576 placements; no two-step (30 degree) gap placement does better than 3 of 458 within 90 degrees of home and about 180 to 280 anywhere.** The width of the gap decides it, not the travel: a strut can stop 15 degrees short of a full turn and still reach every state.

**The working stances rule out most of the 59 (my reading).** The gaps must not sit in the angles the struts use for normal posing, or posing across them would need the long way round: strut A's working stances are 0 to 85 degrees below horizontal toward its side (absolute angles 275 to 360; 30 to -55 degrees from its home at 330) and strut B's are absolute 180 to 265 (-30 to +55 from home at 210; `chassis-simulation.md`). The workable pairs are: A's gap after 0 to 45 degrees from home with B's after 90 to 165; A's after 195 to 255 with B's after 315 to 15; or A's after 330 or 345 with B's after 90 to 150 (degrees from each strut's home, the gap being the step from that angle to the next 15 degrees on). The second family puts B's gap inside B's working range, and A's gaps at 0 to 30 and at 330 or 345 are inside A's; **only A's gap after 45 (the step from 45 to 60 degrees from home, absolute 15 to 30 degrees, just above horizontal toward its side) is outside A's working range, with B's gap after 105 to 165.**

**Choice (mine, 2026-11-14): a stop with travel of about 345 degrees: strut A's dead sector between absolute 15 and 30 degrees (centred on 22.5 degrees, at most about 10 degrees wide) and strut B's between absolute 345 and 360 degrees (centred on 352.5 degrees), the same 10 degree gap in each.** B's gap after 135 degrees from its home (210) is the step from absolute 345 to 360, outside B's working range of 180 to 265 and inside the table's workable set for A's gap after 45. A pin and tab can leave a dead sector as narrow as the sum of their widths: 1.5 mm for the tab and 1.0 mm for the pin on the boss rim, radius 15 mm, is 2.5 mm, 9.5 degrees (my arithmetic), which fits inside one 15 degree step with margin; the strut boss's rim or the housing flange (radius 13 mm) can carry the stop. **The earlier "firmware must return the struts to within 90 degrees of home" rule is no longer needed for righting;** a firmware soft limit 3 degrees inside each side of the gap, and the rule that a strut never commands a move through its gap, still apply. The cable (item 6) now needs the wrap for 345 degrees: 100 mm of cable, from the table there.
**Why the hard stop at all:** a servo at 0.17 N.m on a 16.5 mm boss pulls a taut wire with about 10 N, about the breaking load of a 30 AWG strand (ASM), so the servo's own torque limit is not a safe protection.
**Checked again on a 5 degree grid (`hardware/strut/stop_gap_fine.py`, 13,836 rest states, 54,064 moves; the grid angles inside a 10 degree dead sector are removed and the moves into or across them blocked):** the design above (A's sector centred on 22.5 degrees, B's on 352.5) strands **0 of 12,269 inverted rest states**, and of the 5,184 placements of the two sectors, **537 strand none**. **But A's 22.5 degrees sits at the edge of the workable range** (the workable centres for strut A, with B near 352.5, are 2.5 to 22.5 degrees; 27.5 strands some), so a few degrees of tolerance in the stop would leave it. **Revised choice: strut A's dead sector centred on 12.5 degrees (about 7.5 to 17.5 absolute) and strut B's centred on 342.5 (about 337.5 to 347.5)**, the middle of the workable ranges (A 2.5 to 22.5 for B in 302.5 to 22.5; B 302.5 to 22.5, so 80 degrees of room for B and 20 for A, centred), each 10 degrees wide at most; both are outside the working ranges (A 275 to 360 below horizontal, B 180 to 265; A's sector starts 7.5 degrees above horizontal). The pin and tab (2.5 mm at a 15 mm radius, 9.5 degrees) fit. **What is not covered:** the search moves one strut by one grid step at a time, as the simulation's righting does, and treats the body's settling as a rolling downhill in the planar model; the real stop's tolerance and the servo's stopping accuracy are not in it.

## 8. The lids and their screws: a finding, and the recommendation

**Finding:** each lid is almost all cell. The cell pocket is about 134.4 x 133.9 mm in a 140 x 140 mm face (`body-redesign.md`), leaving a **rim of only 2.8 mm (sides) and 3.05 mm (top and bottom)**. An M2 pan head is about 3.8 mm across (ASM), so screws **cannot go through the lid face**, and the rim cannot carry a gasket and a screw boss as well.
**Owner's standing decision (2026-11-12, `body-redesign.md`):** for now the lids are not separate components in the design process; the body is one block of forged carbon, and the lid joint, lid screws and board-to-board link wait until the body is more concrete.
**Recommendation (mine) when the lids come back:** retain each lid by **side-entry screws**: M2 screws going in horizontally through the body wall's outer rim into threaded inserts in the lid's edge, on the two faces that have room (the hub faces have the hub hardware in the middle but free corners; the end faces have the window and the cell pocket, so the hub faces are the better choice), eight per lid or so (ASM, to match the gasket path of about 520 mm); **seal with a flat die-cut silicone gasket, 0.5 mm (ASM), on a flange inside the rim**, dust-tight rather than watertight, to match the tabletop use case. **Alternative:** a bonded lid (not removable), which loses the cell-replacement plan the owner asked for (`body-redesign.md`, item 3 of the cell decisions). I do not recommend it.
**The owner's method for the lid's shape (2026-11-14) and a first cut of it (`hardware/body/lid_split.py`, `docs/images/lid-split.png`).** The owner's idea: once every component is placed and its cavities decided, **cut the lid out of the body along the surface equidistant from the lid components (the cells, and the board that rides on the lid) and the body components (everything else)**, a 3D Voronoi boundary that stays as far as it can from both; do it for the top and the bottom lid, overlay the two in the same orientation, and **keep only what they have in common** as the rough lid shape, before fasteners and topology optimisation. My reading: sound as a first cut, and it can be run now and rerun whenever a component moves, since the script takes about a minute (so the "everything decided first" condition makes it a check to repeat, not a step to wait for). **The prototype** works on the 1 mm voxel model of `layout.py`: lid components are the cells (a 134.4 x 133.9 mm, 1.8 mm pocket on each lid face) and the board on that lid; body components are the servos and hub with their clearance, the battery, camera, LED ring, ranging sensor, comms patch and the end-face cells; each point goes to the nearer group by a 3D Euclidean distance transform; to make a lid that lifts straight off, only the part connected to the lid face down each column is kept (a height field). **Results (the design space of each lid, not its material):** the top lid's region is **240 cm3; none of it is undercut** (0%); **the bottom lid mirrored is the same 240 cm3 and the two differ by 0 cm3**, so the identical-lids condition holds exactly for this layout and the intersection is the whole region; the lid is **4 mm deep at its shallowest, 11 mm at the median and 30 mm at its deepest** (down to the mid-plane, in empty regions far from any component); and the split surface is smooth (neighbouring columns differ by more than 3 mm in 0.2% of places). **What the method does not give (my reading):** (1) **the parting line on the outside wanders**: at the outer faces the lid's depth runs from 4 to 27 mm (median 10), so the visible seam and the gasket path would rise and fall round the body; a seal wants a clean, simple path; (2) **the surface in empty space has no structural meaning**: halfway between two components is arbitrary where nothing is there, so the deep parts (up to 30 mm) are only the method's neutral choice; (3) **the end-face cells stop it near the ends**: they span z = 7.7 to 52.3 on the faces, so at the +y and -y faces the parting line must lie between 52.3 and the cells' lid-face pocket, a lid depth under 7.7 mm there; and the camera bezel (z 12 to 48) sits below that. **Recommendation (mine): use the method for the interior, with the outer rim fixed by rule:** a parting line at a constant height round all four sides (about 6 to 7 mm below the lid face, above the end cells' top edge at 52.3, flat so that the gasket is flat), the Voronoi surface only inside that rim, and the lid keeps its board in a pocket on its underside (the board's lower face is 11.6 mm below the face, so the lid is at least that deep over the board). **Then the lid screws and the gasket:** the side-entry screws recommended above need a lid edge at least 4 mm deep at the hub faces, which the rim rule gives. **Done on 2026-11-14 (owner agreed the recommendation): the rim rule, the mass, and a real CAD split.**
- **Rim rule applied** (`lid_split.py`): the parting plane is 6.5 mm below the lid face within 8 mm of the outer faces (7.0 mm on the 1 mm grid) and the Voronoi surface applies inside. **The final lid's design space is 224 cm3; it overlaps no body component; the nearest body component is 2.0 mm from it** (the layout's own clearance is 1 mm); the lid is 7 mm deep at the rim, 10 mm at the median and up to 30 mm in the interior; the cell pocket leaves 4.7 to 5.2 mm of lid under the cells at the rim (6.5 to 7.0 minus the 1.8 mm cell). **The shape is a design space, not a lid (owner, 2026-11-14): it is the maximum space the lid may occupy, to be cut down later by topology optimisation, and mass is not a decision input until that step.** (I had first added shell-mass estimates and drawn a conclusion from them; the owner corrected that and I removed them from `lid_split.py`.)
- **A real CAD split of the design space** (`hardware/body/lid_cad.py`, writes `hardware/body/out/lid.step` and `body_mid.step`; the "lid" in the file names is the lid's design space): the Voronoi region is arbitrary in its interior, so the CAD space is **a stepped, flat-faced simplification of it**: a plate from the face down to the rim plane (7 mm), over the whole 140 x 140 mm footprint, plus **a raised platform under the board** (the board's footprint plus 2 mm, down to 2 mm below the board's lower face, z = 46.4 for the top lid). The bottom lid is its mirror about z = 30 (identical, as the method found). **The lid is 172 cm3 and the body between the lids 831 cm3, one solid each; `lid.step` re-imports as one solid; neither lid overlaps a component, and the nearest component is 1.0 mm away** (the layout's clearance). **Open:** the topology optimisation that will turn the space into the lid (the carbon's real wall thickness and the ribs), the board's pocket and mounting bosses, the rim's gasket groove and the side-entry screw bosses (the 7 mm rim at the hub faces is deep enough for an M2 screw into an insert on the lid edge), the cell pocket's insulating layer, and the board-to-board link across the joint; and **`block.py` now cuts the cavities from the whole block and then splits the result by the two lid design spaces (`lid_cad.py`'s `lid_spaces()`) into three parts, written as `body_lid_top.step`, `body_lid_bottom.step` and `body_middle.step`** (2026-11-14): the top and bottom lids are **136.2 cm3 each (identical, as the method predicted, now with the board cavities and the window and wire holes cut)** and the body between them is **598.3 cm3**; each part re-imports as **one solid** and the three add up to the cut block's 870.7 cm3. (The 172.4 cm3 of the uncut lid space loses 36 cm3 to the cavities that lie in it: the boards, the camera end's window and bezel pockets, the wire holes.) `body_block.step` (the whole cut block) is still written.
- **The fastening details, as stand-ins in the same model (2026-11-14; `lid_cad.py`'s `joint_features()`, cut in `block.py`):** these are the fixed (non-design) features the later topology optimisation must keep, drawn to fit the design spaces, not final.
  - **Gasket groove:** 2 mm wide and 0.8 mm deep in the body's rim face at each lid joint (z = 53 for the top lid, 7 for the bottom), a rectangular loop 3 to 5 mm in from the outer faces (the inner edge of the groove 65 mm from the axis), inboard of the end-face cells (which start 68.2 mm out) and clear of the lid ears; 0.85 cm3 each. A flat silicone gasket or a 1 mm cord sits in it.
  - **The lid's fastening: catches on the lid, cams in the body (the owner's design, 2026-11-14; CAD in `hardware/body/lid_cams.py`, sections in `docs/images/cam-joint-axial.png` and `cam-joint-radial.png`).** This replaces my first fastening (side-entry M2 screws, which only held the lid against lifting and did not press it onto the gasket) and the three ways I then sketched to fix that (a wedge key, a cam bolt, a bell crank). **I withdraw those three sketches and their renders (deleted):** they were rough slices with parts that overlapped and with the surrounding model cut off, so they did not show a mechanism that works. The owner's Minifix-style design replaces them.
    - **How it works (owner's description, with my numbers):** four **catches** per lid (plain pins: a 1.2 mm pin with a round head 2.1 mm across) hang from the lid's ears; four **cams** per lid sit in bores from the hub face, two per face at y = +-40 mm, axis at z = 38.5 mm on the top lid (z = 21.5 on the bottom lid), the catch 10 mm in from the face (|x| = 60). **Unlocked**, each cam (10 mm across, flange 13 mm) stands 0.5 mm proud of the hub face on its own gasket and the lid sits on the seam gasket, 0.4 mm proud of its groove; the lid is lowered so that each catch's head drops into its cam through an entry notch. **Turning the cam 180 degrees from the hub face** (a screwdriver slot in its flange; a dimple marks the throat side) does two things at once: its **T-slot draws the catch's head from 3.4 mm to 3.0 mm from the cam's axis, pulling the lid 0.4 mm down onto the seam gasket (to a hard stop at the rim plane)**, and **its thread (M10 x 1.0, 0.5 mm of advance per half turn) pulls the cam in 0.5 mm, flush with the face, squeezing its own gasket washer (1.3 to 0.8 mm)**. One gasket seals the seam; one seals each cam's hole.
    - **What actually catches the catch (the owner's question, 2026-11-14): the two rails of the cam's T-slot.** The slot in the cam is a T in section: a **chamber** inside the cam (3.4 mm wide along the cam's axis, 2.4 mm across) holds the round head, and a **slit** (1.5 mm wide along the axis) leads from the chamber out through the cam's surface for the pin; the cam material on either side of the slit, outside the chamber, forms **two thin rails (0.95 mm wide each, 0.8 mm thick over the head at the locked end)**. The head, wider than the slit, cannot pass the rails; as the cam turns, the chamber and slit follow a spiral that brings the head nearer the axis, so the rails draw the head, and the lid with it, down. At the throat the slit's rails are cut away by the entry notch, which is how the head goes in and out when unlocked. **My first model of this (earlier today) was wrong: its groove was a plain open channel with no rails, cut right through the cam's surface, so nothing held the head and the cam could not have pulled the lid; the owner's question exposed it.** It needed a larger cam (the rails must lie outside the chamber, 3.4 + 1.2 + 0.8 mm from the axis, so the cam is 10 mm across and not the 8 mm I had) and a smaller head and pin (2.1 and 1.2 mm, not 2.7 and 1.8). Minifix-type cams are about 12 to 15 mm across for this reason.
    - **180 degrees against 90 (the owner's question; my recommendation is 180):** (1) the spiral's slope: the chamber's centre radius falls 0.5 mm over 180 degrees along an arc of about 10 mm, **a slope of about 3 degrees**; over 90 degrees it would be twice as steep (about 6 degrees); both are under the 8.5 degrees at which a wedge of friction 0.15 stops being self-locking, but the gentler slope pulls harder for the same torque (1 / tan of the angle: about 19 times ideal at 3 degrees, 10 at 6); (2) **the thread: 0.5 mm of advance per half turn at M10 x 1.0 would be only 0.25 mm in a quarter turn, too little to squeeze the cam's gasket from 1.3 to 0.8 mm; a quarter turn would need a 2 mm pitch (M10 x 2 is not a fine thread and a coarse one loosens more easily)**; (3) the owner's point: 180 degrees gives more circumference to work with. **So 180 degrees.** The seat is reached after 144 degrees (the head's seated radius, 3.0 mm, is where the spiral passes it) and the last 36 degrees are a dwell that takes up tolerance against the hard stop.
    - **Checks (`lid_cams.py`, both states):** the six parts of one joint (body, lid, catch, cam, seam gasket, cam gasket) **do not interfere** (overlap under 0.5 mm3), each is one solid, and the head has about 0.15 mm of clearance in the chamber. **Retention test: lifting the head 0.3 mm and 0.6 mm toward the lid in the locked state makes it overlap the cam's rails (0.07 and 0.31 mm3), so the cam blocks it; in the unlocked state it overlaps nothing, so it can rise out through the entry notch.** `lid_cad.py` and `block.py` cut the eight cam bores (10.2 mm), their counterbores (13.4 mm) and the catch holes: **the lids stay 137.0 cm3 each (identical, one solid each) and the body between them is 583.5 cm3, one solid; the three parts add up to the cut block less the two seam grooves.**
    - **The cam's head: no slot (owner's concern, 2026-11-14: an object in the environment could catch a slot's edge, push on it, turn the cam and unlock the lid; my analysis and the change).** A slot is the worst case: its edges reach almost to the rim (about 6 mm from the axis), so a snagging object has a long lever and a sharp edge to push. **What I changed:** the head is now a **smooth flush cap with a pin-in-hex socket (4 mm across the flats) in the middle**: nothing outside to grip, and an object engaging the socket has a lever arm of at most 2.3 mm (its corners) instead of 6, and cannot enter it unless it is shaped to clear the central pin. **Drive types considered (vendor descriptions, not tests; [machinemfg](https://shop.machinemfg.com/types-of-security-bolt-heads-a-comprehensive-analysis), [AFT](https://aftfasteners.com/security-screws)):** pin-in-Torx and pin-in-hex (a central pin blocks an ordinary bit or key; pin-in-Torx takes a little more torque and is rated higher security, pin-in-hex is simpler to model and find), tri-wing (minimal security, rejected), spanner or snake-eye (two small holes: medium security, but two holes at some radius give a poking object more purchase than one central socket, so rejected), one-way screws (cannot be reversed with any tool: **unsuitable, the cam must unlock**) and break-away nuts (same). **The recommendation: pin-in-Torx (a T20 or similar) if a driver is easy to get, pin-in-hex 4 mm for now; the CAD has the hex form.** **Second layer: a breakaway torque, so that even a tool-shaped push cannot turn it.** My estimate of the worst accidental torque: a 20 N push at the flange's edge (6.5 mm) is 0.13 N.m, and a 20 N object dragging across the smooth flush face with a friction coefficient of 0.3 is 0.04 N.m; **the target is a breakaway torque of at least 0.4 N.m (my choice, three times the worst case)**, about 5 N on an 80 mm key, easy for a person and far more than any bump gives. How to get it: **a prevailing-torque patch on the M10 thread** (a nylon patch or a microencapsulated adhesive patch: the vendors say a nylon patch can be applied to screws down to M1.2, that the torque to seat the screw is the plain seating torque plus the prevailing torque, and that microencapsulated patches give a lower assembly torque but a higher breakaway torque; sources: [Penn Engineering](https://www.pencomsf.com/wp-content/uploads/technical-reference-files/installation_torque_screws_non-metallic_locking.pdf), [Nylok summary](https://aftfasteners.com/threadlockers-nylon-patches), [Assembly Magazine](https://www.assemblymag.com/articles/85609-do-your-fasteners-need-a-preapplied-thread-locker)); I did not find how many unlock and relock cycles such a patch survives (the lid is opened for service, so this matters: a single-use adhesive patch would need replacing; a nylon patch typically lasts a few cycles, my recollection, unchecked) nor its value on an M10 thread; **the value must be tested on the real part.** **Alternatives if a patch is not enough:** a spring-loaded ball detent in the cam's flange that drops into dimples in the counterbore at the locked and unlocked angles (reusable and positive, but it needs room in the 0.2 mm radial gap round the flange, so the flange would have to be larger), or a thin plastic snap cap over the socket (hides the drive, a part to lose). **Orientation:** with no slot or dimple the cam's angle is not visible; a laser-etched tick on the rim (no relief to catch) or the hex's orientation would show it (the hex looks the same every 60 degrees, so a tick is needed). **Checks:** `lid_cams.py` still passes: no interference, the head is blocked at the locked end and free at the throat.
    - **Service access (seen in the new hub-face render, 2026-11-14):** the struts sweep a 70 mm disc about the hub and cover the cam heads at many angles (in the default 25 degree stance an arm hides the bottom lid's heads; the top lid's two show), so **to open a lid the struts have to be parked clear of the heads**: the heads are at y = +-40 mm and the arms are 24 to 30 mm wide, so with both struts of a side pointing straight up and straight down (along z, 180 degrees apart; the stance table reaches 85 degrees, and the stop gaps leave those angles free, `item 7`) every head is clear. A firmware **service pose** that does this is worth having (my suggestion), and the stand-in for a rover that cannot move its struts is to remove a wheel.
    - **Not modelled or not decided:** the thread (plain cylinders); the cam's material (stainless or brass for the rails, which are 0.8 mm thick; the core between the chamber and the cam's axis is about 1.7 mm across); the catch's thread into an insert in the ear (the pin is shown seated in a blind hole); the bottom lid's mirror cams (placed by `lid_cad.py`, not drawn in `lid_cams.py`); the cam heads on the hub faces are eight round smooth caps 13 mm across with a pin-in-hex socket, flush; drawn in gunmetal like the window bezel's screws (the finish is the owner's choice).

**Also needed for the removable lid (2026-11-14, `hardware/body/harness.py`):** each lid's board connects to the body's wiring through spring contacts in a pocket on each side of the lid's platform (|x| 37 to 49, 12 mm deep, 3.2 mm high, from y = -5; 24.6 mm long on both sides, identical on both lids), pressing sideways on pads on the platform's side face, so they part when the lid lifts straight off. The strut motors' wires reach the lower lid's pockets through the 2 mm wall hole at z = 11 and a 3.2 mm channel (the wheel drivers are on the processor board, lower lid); between the two lids only VBAT, V7.4 and ground (two contacts each) and three slow signals cross, through body wiring. Contact count, partition and reasons are in `harness.py`; **the contact part is chosen (2026-11-14, `hardware/body/contact_fit.py`): Mill-Max series 854 horizontal SMT strips, 854-22-018-40-601101 (18 positions, with mounting pegs) in every pocket.** Why this one: the piston moves parallel to the board, so the strip lies flat in the 3.2 mm pocket (0.8 mm board + 2.21 mm profile = 3.01 mm: **the board must be 0.8 mm or thinner**) and its pistons press sideways on the lid's pads; one strip per pocket (up to 20 positions in stock); 2 A continuous and 3 A peak, 20 mOhm max, 25 g to 60 g spring force over a 0.7 mm mid-stroke, 1,000,000 cycles. **The pocket had to deepen from 6 to 12 mm** (the strip is 10.9 mm from piston tip to tail end; `lid_cad.py` updated, lids unchanged at 137.0 cm3, body middle 577.1). Forces: about 10 N on each side at mid stroke (the sides cancel; no vertical load on the gasket), up to 6 N of friction while a lid is lifted off (ESTIMATE, mu 0.3). **Pads on the lid platform's side face (ASM): 0.9 mm wide, 3 mm tall or more, chamfered lead-in, hard gold 30 microinch over nickel** (the pistons slide along them on every removal; soft ENIG would wear). To be measured: contact resistance and wear over 20 lid cycles (to-be-measured item 3).

## 8a. Body-side wire routes (2026-11-14; `hardware/body/wire_routes.py`, channels cut in `block.py`)

**Method.** A shortest-path search through the design block's free volume (keep-in minus servos and hub minus every placed component, 2 mm clear of all of them, a 2 mm grid, bends penalised), from each pocket's wire pads (|x| = 50, the pocket's middle in y) to where the wires go; each route is a list of axis-aligned segments. Each route was then **checked independently at 1 mm**: a square channel of the bundle's width swept along it touches 0 obstacle voxels in all 13 bundles. Wires: 30 AWG silicone (0.6 mm over the insulation, the size `cable_check.py` uses) for signals, 28 AWG (0.8 mm) where a net carries power; channel side from a 50% fill plus 0.4 mm, at least 3 mm.

**Result (every bundle has a route; lengths are Manhattan along the channel).** Camera end 64 mm (10 wires, under the camera board's lower edge); LED ring supply 99 mm (2); link signals 57 mm (3, up the free corridor at |x| about 49, just outside the servo faces); battery to the upper pocket 112 mm (12, 3.9 mm channel); antenna end 129 mm (5, to the XBee carrier board at the centre of the rear bay); power across to the lower - pocket 173 mm (8, the longest: it has to pass the battery at the front of the body); three solar strings 75 to 160 mm; the servo chain 77 + 73 + 159 + 77 mm. Total 1.4 m of route, 6.9 g of wire (the layout allows 15 g for wiring, connectors and board links; the strut-motor wires are separate). All channels together take 20.4 cm3 of the block, and with the pockets the body middle is 564.4 cm3 (lids 136.8 and 136.7: the bottom lid's cell wires run inside its plate, so the lids differ by 0.1 cm3 from this).

**Changes this made.** (1) **The three link signals moved to the + side of the upper lid** (they were on its - side while the lower lid has them on its + side, which would have run the wires across the body): the upper + pocket now has 18 contacts and the - pocket 4, and **one part serves every pocket: the 18-position strip** (`harness.py`, `contact_fit.py`). (2) Pockets are 24.6 mm on both sides.

**Assumptions to replace (ASM):** the servo connectors at each servo's end opposite its output shaft (the STS3215's real connector position was not checked); the bottom lid's cell wires leaving at its - edge at y = 4 and the end-face cells' wires at their pockets' middles; the servo chain's order (B+ to B- to A- to A+) and its three wires; wire sizes and masses per metre. **The XBee is now placed (later 2026-11-14, `layout.py`, `hardware-spec.md` last section).** Not done: the fan-out of each bundle along its 24.6 mm pocket, bundle twisting, connector bodies, and the real shell's draft and ribs.

## 10. Values estimated instead of tested (method of 2026-11-14)

Purchasing is deferred, so values that need a physical test are estimated with a range, designed to, and tagged ESTIMATE until a test replaces them (`CLAUDE.md`). To be measured later is listed with each.
- **Lid cam breakaway torque (`hardware/body/cam_estimate.py`; ESTIMATE, inputs are assumptions):** gasket washer 51 mm2 compressed 38% gives 26 to 102 N on the cam; thread and gasket friction alone give **0.06 to 0.65 N.m, so the 0.4 N.m target is not met at the low end without a patch**. With a nylon prevailing-torque patch (0.3 to 1.5 N.m, remembered order of magnitude) the new cam breaks away at 0.37 to 2.2 N.m, and 0.25 N.m in the worst case after reuse (patch down to 60%). **Design rule: specify the patch at 0.6 N.m or more first-on prevailing torque** (computed minimum 0.55 for 0.4 N.m after reuse at the worst friction corner); the new cam then stays under about 2.4 N.m, inside the 4 mm hex socket's assumed 3 N.m. To be measured: breakaway with a torque screwdriver (10 cams, 20 cycles). Not bounded: the patch's real torque and its wear (supplier data needed).
- **Sapphire window and the ranging sensor (`hardware/body/window_estimate.py`; ESTIMATE, the emitter-to-receiver separation is backed out from ST's gap rule, not from a datasheet):** uncoated sapphire reflects about 1.8 times the light of glass (14.3% against 8.2% total) and costs 13% of the round-trip signal (about 7% of the range; harmless at the 12 to 16 cm that matters). A direct specular reflection from the outer surface would need the gap to be under about 0.53 mm to miss the receiver if the emitter-to-receiver separation is 1.0 mm (0.92 mm if 1.5 mm), so **the 0.5 mm gap was marginal: the sensor box in `layout.py` moved 0.2 mm toward the glass (y 66.2 to 67.7), a 0.3 mm gap**, with the black foam ring pressed against the glass. **Decision rule:** build with uncoated sapphire; if a later bench test of the sensor's crosstalk histogram (1 to 3 cm behind the real window) is over the limit, use AR-coated sapphire, then 0.5 mm glass. Not estimable: the diffuse crosstalk and the sensor's absolute crosstalk limit (not found).
- **Spring contacts on the power paths (`hardware/body/contact_estimate.py`; ESTIMATE: 10 to 100 mOhm per contact over its life is a remembered typical range, to confirm in the datasheet):** with two contacts per power net, the battery path (3.5 A peak at 3.0 V) would drop up to 0.35 V (12% of the cell) and each contact would carry 1.75 A and dissipate about 0.3 W aged: not acceptable. **Counts now (`harness.py`): VBAT and ground at the battery 6 each, the 7.4 V rail 3 forward and 3 return, the 3.3 V converter's VBAT 2.** Aged-worst drop at the peak: 117 mV on the battery path (3.9%), 133 mV on the 7.4 V paths (1.8%); at most 0.58 A and 34 mW per contact on the battery path. Pockets: lower lid 20.8 mm (+ side) and 25.9 mm (- side), upper lid 20.8 and 10.6; both lids use the longer (identical lids). **Follow-up worth considering (recommendation, not done): put the cell protection IC and FET pair on a small board at the battery rather than on the power board,** so that its under-voltage trip sees the cell, not the cell less the contact drop. To be measured: contact resistance new and after 20 lid cycles (four-wire), temperature rise at 2 A.
