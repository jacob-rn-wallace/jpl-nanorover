# Electronics concept: the spirit of that era of robotics

Status as of 2026-11-12. This records the owner's guiding principle for the electronics, what was found while applying it, and what is still open. SRC = a cited source, ASM = my assumption, unverified items are marked. Nothing here is built or bought (purchasing is deferred indefinitely).

## The principle (owner, 2026-11-12)

The project is an interpretation, not a replica, but **it keeps the spirit of the era of robotics the nanorover belongs to, "things like the MIT Handy Board and the CMUcam". The owner calls this a strong part of the interpretation.** **Scope (owner, 2026-11-12): the spirit governs parts selection only; it does not change how the rover functions. It is still a teleoperated rover with a camera, like an iRobot PackBot (also a robot of the era).** My reading of what that means for choices (to be confirmed): prefer small, simple parts that a single person can understand, with a clear lineage to mid-1990s to early-2000s hobby, academic or JPL robotics: one microcontroller board with motor drivers and sensor inputs (the Handy Board pattern), smart sensors on a serial link (the CMUcam pattern), simple radios and a modest data rate. Avoid Linux-class boards in the core of the rover, not because they cannot do the job but because they are not in the spirit and they cost power.

**Wider research on what that spirit is: `docs/era-of-robotics.md`** (a timeline of the scene, Blikstein's framework, a characterisation, how today differs, and candidate design rules).

## What was found for the processor

- The earlier idea (a saved Gemini conversation in the private repo, `previous-context/`, an AI summary and not a source) was the **Microchip PIC32MX250F128B** as the "architectural twin" of the Synova R3000: both are MIPS, and underclocked to 10 MHz the PIC32 would match about 15 DMIPS. **The 15 DMIPS figure is unverified** (no rating found). Under the interpretation mindset the twin argument carries no weight, but **the lineage still holds: R3000 (the planned flight processor) to MIPS32, and PIC (the Widget boards).**
- PIC32MX250F128B (SRC, distributor listings and the Microchip datasheet family DS60001168): MIPS32 M4K core up to 50 MHz (many listings 40 MHz), 128 KB flash, 32 KB RAM, I2C, SPI, USB, UART; SOIC, SSOP, SPDIP and QFN packages. Lifecycle status not found. It has no camera interface and no radio, and 32 KB of RAM is too little to hold an image.
- Mongoose-V (SRC): radiation-hardened, R3000-compatible, 10 or 15 MHz grades (12 MHz on New Horizons), first flown on EO-1 in November 2000.
- A Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W (65 x 30 mm, about 10 g, boots in about 30 s) idles at about 0.6 to 0.7 W by two sources (280 mA at 5 V by a third), several times the 0.105 W the power study assumed for the controller with its radio idle.

## What was found for the camera

- The Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 needs a Linux-class board with a CSI port, and a 26 mm window passes only 84% of its field (`docs/body-redesign.md`, optical stack).
- **Smart camera modules (found by search, mostly retailer listings):** Pixy2 (CMUcam5): MT9M114 1296 x 976 sensor, dual-core NXP LPC4330, 60 x 40 degree M12 lens, UART, SPI, I2C and USB, 38 x 42 x 15 mm, about 10 g, 140 mA typical; it reports tracked objects, and I believe raw video leaves it only over USB (unverified). OpenMV Cam H7 Plus: STM32H743 at 480 MHz, OV5640 5 MP, 32 MB SDRAM, about 45 x 36 mm and 17 g (single source), UART, SPI, I2C, CAN, MicroPython; one retailer lists it as retired, so the current OpenMV range needs checking. Arducam Mini 5MP Plus: JPEG output and a frame buffer in the module, 34 x 24 mm board, M12 holder, SPI and I2C, 3.3 to 5 V, no on-board vision. XIAO ESP32S3 Sense: camera plus ESP32-S3 with Wi-Fi in 21 x 17.8 mm; it would replace the PIC32.
- **With an M12 lens the window problem goes away (ray trace, ASM sensor 3.67 x 2.73 mm, F2.0):** a 3.6 mm lens gives 54 x 41.5 degrees with no rays lost through the 25 mm plate and 26 mm window, 4.0 mm gives 49 x 38, 4.5 mm gives 44 x 34, 2.8 mm loses 7% at the plate and 12% at the window. An M12 lens also focuses close, which suits a lamp meant for close subjects.

## How the principle ranks the options (mine, to be confirmed)

- **Controller:** a PIC32MX board in the Handy Board pattern (one board: four wheel-motor drivers, a serial port for the servo bus, analog inputs, expansion header) keeps the MIPS and PIC lineage and removes the Linux board; an 8051-class part would echo the prototype; a 68HC11-line part (NXP S12) would echo the Handy Board itself. The PIC32 is the best compromise (two of the three lineages, modern, cheap).
- **Camera (revised for teleoperation):** the camera is the operator's eyes, so it must deliver images or video; the Pixy2 (a tracker) drops out as the main camera, and the choice is between a composite-video camera with an analog video transmitter (almost no processing in the rover) and an imaging smart module (OpenMV or Arducam) with a modest digital radio; see `docs/era-of-robotics.md`, section 5. The earlier ranking follows, for the module lineage:
- **Camera lineage:** the literal CMUcam is the Pixy2, but it is a tracker, not an imaging camera; OpenMV is the CMUcam's spiritual successor (it keeps the old site's pages) and does both imaging and tracking; the Arducam is the simplest imaging part but has the least of the era's character; the Pi camera and the ESP32 are the furthest from it. The CMUcam1 and 2 era also sent small low-resolution frames, which fits a slow link such as the original's 4800 bit/s radio (my reasoning).
- **Radio:** a simple serial radio at a modest data rate rather than Wi-Fi; not yet looked at.

## Decision: the camera and the link (owner, 2026-11-12) **(SUPERSEDED the same day by the digital decision below; kept as the record of what was found)**

**The video route is analog: a composite-video camera and an analog video transmitter, with a separate low-rate command and telemetry radio. "If there's a dim light situation, you can just turn on the headlamp"**, so the camera does not need to be a low-light type. The controller does not touch video; it handles driving, the struts, the lamp, power and the command radio. The Raspberry Pi camera, the Linux board, the smart camera modules (Pixy2, OpenMV, Arducam) and the ESP32 are all out.

Found (SRC, listings and datasheets by search, not read in full): the popular small analog camera is the RunCam Micro Swift line (19 x 19 x 19 mm, 5.6 g, 1/3 inch Sony CCD, 600 TVL, PAL or NTSC, 5 to 36 V, 60 mA at 12 V or 120 mA at 5 V), **but it comes with 145 to 165 degree lenses (M8, or M12 on the later version), far too wide for the window and plate**. Board cameras with an M12 mount and composite output suit the optical stack: the Watec WAT-1100MBD (1/3.2 inch CMOS, over 750 TVL, M12, 1 V composite, 60 mA at 12 V), generic 30 x 30 mm 600 TVL boards (12 V, 90 mA maximum, sensor and lens mount not stated), the Marshall V-1217-PCB (32 x 32 x 30 mm, 1/3 inch CCD, black and white, fixed 3.7 mm lens). **With a 1/3 inch sensor the lens must be about 6 mm (43.6 x 33.4 degrees) to pass the 26 mm window and the polarising plate with no loss; 4 mm loses about 10%** (`hardware/body/optics.py`, section 5).

Video transmitter (SRC, forum and review pages): 5.8 GHz is today's analog FPV standard; 1.2 to 1.3 GHz penetrates better and goes further but needs a large antenna (a 400 mW 1.3 GHz unit weighs 58 g plus a 17 g antenna and draws about 0.7 A at 12 V); a 5.8 GHz unit with an adjustable output draws 90 to 560 mA at 12 V across its range (one example, 25 mW to 1.6 W). No current or weight found for the small low-power units, and nothing for 2.4 GHz or for indoor use. **Licensing (US, from AMA and FCC-related pages, to be checked against 47 CFR parts 15 and 97): most FPV transmitters are not certified for licence-free use and need an amateur licence; a Part 15 certified, unmodified unit does not.** **The 5.8 GHz power limits quoted by one vendor conflict with themselves.** The operator also needs a receiver and a monitor or capture device (not part of the rover).

Consequences (mine): the power budget changes (a camera at 0.5 to 0.7 W, a transmitter at roughly 0.5 to 2 W, not yet known; both want 5 to 12 V, which the servos' 12 V rail can supply); the radio list is now two links, a video transmitter and a command radio (serial, modest rate, in the era's manner), the second not yet looked at; analog video is the period's way of doing it, though 5.8 GHz FPV parts are modern.

## Decision: digital low-bitrate video (owner, 2026-11-12, replaces the analog route above) **(its slow-scan reading and its 900 MHz radio are superseded by the 15 fps section below)**

**The owner reversed the analog choice and chose digital video at a low bitrate** ("in that case, let's go digital with the video and have it be a low bitrate"). The reason was not stated; my reading is the licensing and transmitter questions found for the analog route. The headlamp still covers dim light.

**What low bitrate means (my arithmetic; the frame sizes are ASM, to be measured on a real camera):** the video is slow-scan, a JPEG frame every second or two, not smooth video. Time per frame, before protocol overhead and retransmission:

| Frame (ASM) | 9.6 kbit/s | 38.4 kbit/s | 115.2 kbit/s |
|---|---|---|---|
| 160 x 120, about 4 KB | 3.3 s | 0.8 s | 0.3 s |
| 320 x 240, about 10 KB | 8.3 s | 2.1 s | 0.7 s |
| 640 x 480, about 25 KB | 21 s | 5.2 s | 1.7 s |

Sources for frame sizes are rough (SRC, by search): a tutorial puts QVGA at 10 to 30 KB and VGA at 20 to 50 KB, an old Canon manual gives 4 to 7 KB at 320 x 240; capture frames on the real camera. **The rover drives at about 40 mm/s (`docs/drivetrain-spec.md`, estimated from the videos at 20 to 50 mm/s), so a frame every one or two seconds is 4 to 8 cm of travel, which a person can drive by.** The original's planned radio was 4800 bit/s.

**One link for everything:** a modest digital radio can carry the video, the commands and the telemetry, so the separate command radio of the analog route is not needed (the MPRS robot also used one radio for both; Nomad used two). Found (SRC): the Digi XBee-PRO 900HP, 902 to 928 MHz, 200 kbit/s RF rate (about 105.5 kbit/s throughput in point-to-multipoint), a software-selectable serial rate, about 305 m indoor or urban and 6.5 km outdoor line of sight at 200 kbit/s (610 m and 15.5 km at 10 kbit/s), described as FCC approved for the US; its current draw and size were not found. **Licensing (SRC: 47 CFR 15.247 text found by search): digitally modulated systems in 902 to 928 MHz, 2400 to 2483.5 MHz and 5725 to 5850 MHz may transmit up to 1 W with a 6 dB bandwidth of at least 500 kHz, and an antenna gain above 6 dBi requires less power; a certified module should therefore need no amateur licence, but the modular-approval conditions were not checked.** SSDV (slow scan digital video, from the balloon community) is the usual way to send a JPEG over a slow link: 256-byte packets, each holding independent blocks of the image so a lost packet leaves a gap, not a ruined picture; forward error correction is not clearly part of the base format.

**Camera options come back (all with a JPEG output and an M12 lens, so the optical stack stays workable):**
- **A serial JPEG camera, the most CMUcam-like:** the Adafruit TTL serial camera (VC0706): 3.3 V UART, 1/4 inch 0.3 MP CMOS, 32 x 32 mm, 38400 baud by default, a 160 x 120 option; its miniature variant is retired at one retailer and the current stock is unknown; current and field of view not found.
- **Arducam Mini SPI cameras (2 MP and 5 MP):** JPEG with a frame buffer in the module, the PIC32 only moves bytes; the simplest current part.
- **OpenMV:** the smart one (JPEG on board, serial out, can even own the radio); heavier, more power, one retailer lists the H7 Plus as retired.
- Sensors are 1/4 inch class, so **a 3.6 to 4.5 mm M12 lens passes the 26 mm window and the polarising plate with no loss** (54 x 41.5 degrees at 3.6 mm, 49 x 38 at 4.0, 44 x 34 at 4.5; `optics.py`, section 3 of its output history, or the table in `docs/body-redesign.md`). The analog 1/3 inch route needed 6 mm.

**Architecture that follows (mine):** a PIC32 as the only controller; a camera module with JPEG output; one modest digital radio (a certified 900 MHz or 2.4 GHz module) carrying slow-scan video, commands and telemetry; the operator's station a PC with the matching radio. No Linux board, no video transmitter, no second radio.

## Refinement: at least 15 frames per second (owner, 2026-11-12) **(WITHDRAWN the same day when the owner chose the map-and-stills view below; kept as the record of what was found)**

**"At a certain point, if the framerate is low enough it's no longer video. I feel like it should be at least 15 fps."** So the slow-scan reading above is dropped: the video is at least 15 frames per second, and the bitrate follows from the frame size.

**Bitrate at 15 fps (my arithmetic, MJPEG; the frame sizes are ASM and rough, from a tutorial, a Canon manual and an Arducam post, to be measured):**

| Frame | JPEG size (ASM) | Bitrate at 15 fps |
|---|---|---|
| 160 x 120 | 1.5 to 4 KB | 180 to 480 kbit/s |
| 320 x 240 | 3 to 10 KB | 360 to 1,200 kbit/s |
| 640 x 480 | 20 to 50 KB | 2.4 to 6 Mbit/s |

So **"low bitrate" with 15 fps means roughly 0.2 to 1.2 Mbit/s of JPEG, which is low only against modern video.** The 64 to 128 kbit/s of an ISDN video call (H.263, QCIF) needs a real inter-frame codec, and I found **no MCU-class module with a hardware H.264 or H.263 encoder** (the ESP32-S3 encodes in software at 320 x 240, 11 fps at best; the ESP32-P4 has hardware H.264; the Conexant CX93510 and the C328 serial board give JPEG only), so that route needs a Linux-class SoC.

**What it rules out (SRC where stated):**
- **The 900 MHz serial modems** such as the XBee-PRO 900HP (200 kbit/s RF, about 105 kbit/s throughput) cannot carry even 160 x 120 at 15 fps; 802.15.4 at 250 kbit/s is marginal at best.
- **Serial JPEG cameras** (the Adafruit VC0706 and similar) at 38.4 to 115.2 kbaud: 115.2 kbaud is 11.5 KB/s, 3 to 6 fps of 160 x 120 JPEG; 15 fps needs about 0.3 to 0.6 Mbaud of UART or more.
- **OpenMV with its own Wi-Fi shield:** OpenMV measured the shield at about 250 kbit/s and a user saw 5 fps; a 320 x 240 JPEG over 115.2 kbaud UART was about 1 fps (a forum estimate); a faster UART to another processor would be needed.

**What can do it:**
- **A 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi class link.** This is also the era's own answer: NASA Ames's Nomad (1997) used 2.4 GHz spread-spectrum Ethernet, and the MPRS Urban Robot sent video at 15 to 20 frames per second over a 2.4 GHz Breezecom radio, one link for video and commands. **Microchip MRF24WG0MA** (SRC, datasheet via search): 802.11b/g, SPI up to 10 MHz, application throughput 4,500 kbit/s, certified FCC, IC and ETSI as a module, designed for PIC32 parts with Microchip's TCP/IP stack; its real throughput on a PIC32MX250 (32 KB of RAM, 40 to 50 MHz) is not known, the stack's benchmarks I found are for older modules and bigger parts, and the stack's small buffers are a likely limit; **this needs a bench test, and a PIC32 with more RAM may be needed**.
- **A camera module with enough SPI bandwidth:** the Arducam Mini (SPI at 8 MHz, 8 MB frame buffer) can move 15 fps of QVGA JPEG in bandwidth terms (45 to 150 KB/s against about 1 MB/s); Arducam claims 30 fps at 3 KB per QVGA frame (marketing), a third-party guide estimates 10 to 15 fps for VGA over 8 MHz SPI (unverified); the per-frame overhead on a small MCU is unknown, so test it.
- **A self-contained camera and Wi-Fi module of the ESP32-CAM kind:** reported 20 to 25 fps at QVGA over 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi (a blog table and a user test, quality 12); the PIC32 would then only drive the rover and be linked by serial. It is a modern IoT module and less in the era's spirit, and the lens options for the optical stack (M12) are not established.

**The architectures left (mine):**
1. **All Microchip, one MCU:** a PIC32 (probably with more RAM than the MX250), an Arducam SPI camera, the MRF24WG0MA Wi-Fi module. Best in the era's spirit, with a real risk on throughput and on the PIC32 code (the Arducam libraries target Arduino and STM32, not PIC32).
2. **PIC32 plus a self-contained camera and Wi-Fi module:** proven frame rate, two processors, less era character.
3. A real-codec route (about 100 kbit/s): needs an SoC, not recommended.

**Consequences:** the radio is now a certified 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi module (licence-free by the rule text under 47 CFR 15.247, 1 W, certified module assumed); its current draw is not found (the earlier power study assumed 0.65 W for a transmitting radio, ASM); the operator's end is a PC on the same Wi-Fi.

## Decision: a map-and-stills operator view instead of continuous video, with the SLAM on the operator's console (owner, 2026-11-12)

**Confirmed by the owner (2026-11-12): the SLAM is outsourced to the operator's console, off the rover.** The rover does not stream video and does not run SLAM; it samples its sensors in a cyclic loop and sends compact data (ranges, strut angles, wheel counts, inertial data) to the console, which builds and shows a low-fidelity 3D model of the rover and its surroundings; on request the rover sends a high-resolution still at roughly SSTV speed. The 15 fps requirement is withdrawn. The text below is the analysis made when the idea was proposed.

**The owner's idea:** "much better than video": the rover normally sends only SLAM data, so the operator sees a low-fidelity 3D representation of the rover and its surroundings; on request the rover sends a high-resolution image at about SSTV speeds. The owner favours the OpenMV camera for it and believes it matches Sojourner's control scheme because Sojourner used a cyclic executive. **If adopted, the 15 fps requirement above falls away.**

**Checking the Sojourner claim (SRC: JPL's Pathfinder software page, a Wikipedia comparison of Mars rover computers, an excerpt of Morrison and Nguyen's paper on the rover's software, archived JPL pages on the Rover Control Workstation):**
- **True:** Sojourner's software was a custom cyclic executive, not a multitasking operating system; its main loop dispatched periodic functions (thermal management, health checks) from software timers; the processor limits meant the rover could not drive its motors and run its radio at the same time, which made multitasking a poor fit. The processor is an Intel 8085 at 100 KIPS by JPL's page and an 80C85 at 2 MHz by Wikipedia. This is a good precedent for **one small controller with a bare cyclic loop**, which matches the parts rules of `docs/era-of-robotics.md`.
- **True, as an operator interface:** the Rover Control Workstation gave operators a virtual-reality view of the surface from any angle, with a rover model that they steered on the terrain to create waypoints or goals; the commands were built into sequences and uplinked through the lander, about 12 hours of preparation in 1997, executed the next day.
- **Different from the proposal:** Sojourner's 3D terrain came from the **lander's stereo camera, built on the ground**, not from SLAM on the rover; the rover did not send a map; it reported telemetry and images, and **ran simple onboard navigation behaviours (hazard avoidance) toward commanded goals**, with pose from wheel odometry, accelerometers and a z-axis rate sensor. So the cyclic executive says how the rover is programmed; it does not by itself support SLAM on the rover.

**SLAM at this scale (SRC and my reading):**
- OpenMV's `find_displacement` is FFT phase correlation (translation by default, rotation and scale only weakly): 30 fps at 64 x 64 on the M7 (a forum report), 15 fps with rotation, limited by memory (two FFTs of the image); an OpenMV developer says the project has no pose-estimation framework, so **full visual odometry or SLAM is not supported out of the box**. The OpenMV product lineup (H7 Plus retired at one retailer, newer boards) was not found.
- The usual pattern for SLAM on small robots is SLAM on a Pi-class board with a microcontroller for the motors (TurtleBot3); a student ESP32 project fused two lidars, an IMU and steppers by Kalman filter as a sensor node; sparse visual SLAM is the kind that fits a microcontroller; **no figures for RAM or CPU were found**.
- Sensor candidate for 3D (SRC, STMicroelectronics): the VL53L5CX multizone time-of-flight sensor, 4 x 4 or 8 x 8 zones, up to 4 m, up to 60 Hz, about 63 to 65 degrees diagonal, 2.8 to 3.3 V; package size and current not found.
- **This project has no IMU and no wheel encoders yet:** the strut spec says "not modelled: the encoders"; the wheel motor (Pololu #3068) is not described with one in the docs; the Feetech STS3215 servos report their own position (my knowledge, not in the docs), so strut angles are known. **Sojourner's odometry came from wheel odometry plus inertial sensors, and a rover that postures on struts and slips on loose ground will have worse odometry** (my reading).

**A variant I would put forward (mine): the rover sends the inputs to a map, and the operator's PC builds the 3D model**, as Sojourner's ground software did: compact range frames from a ToF sensor, strut angles, wheel counts and inertial data, a few hundred bytes per update; the rover stays a cyclic-executive sensor server and the mapping runs on the PC, where SLAM is routine. It fits the OpenMV only as a camera, not as a mapper.

**Bandwidth (my arithmetic):** an 8 x 8 ToF frame is about 128 bytes of distances plus a header, so even 5 updates a second with pose is about 8 kbit/s; **a still at SSTV speed** (SSTV, SRC: Robot 36 sends a 320 x 240 colour frame in 36 s, Martin M1 and Scottie S1 take 114 s and 110 s at 320 x 256):

| JPEG (ASM size) | 4.8 kbit/s (the original's radio) | 9.6 kbit/s | 38.4 kbit/s | 100 kbit/s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25 KB (about 640 x 480) | 42 s | 21 s | 5 s | 2 s |
| 60 KB (about 1024 x 768) | 100 s | 50 s | 12.5 s | 4.8 s |
| 150 KB (about 1600 x 1200, the Arducam post's figure) | 250 s | 125 s | 31 s | 12 s |

So at the original's 4800 bit/s a 25 to 60 KB image arrives at SSTV speed; a 5 MP frame would not.

**Consequences if adopted (mine):** the 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi requirement goes, and a **modest 900 MHz serial radio** (such as the XBee-PRO 900HP, 200 kbit/s RF, about 105 kbit/s throughput, FCC approved) comes back, in the era's manner and with more range; the PIC32MX250 is enough (no streaming stack); the headlamp is needed only for stills, saving power; the camera sits idle between requests; the operator sees a model, so driving becomes goal-directed (waypoints) with the rover avoiding hazards by simple reflexes, as Sojourner did, **which is a change in function from the PackBot-like live driving the owner described earlier the same day**.

**Open (owner):** (1) who builds the map, the rover or the operator's PC; (2) goal-directed driving with onboard hazard reflexes against live driving by the model alone; (3) the ranging sensor and inertial sensor, and wheel encoders; (4) the image size and the radio; (5) OpenMV as a camera only, or also for visual odometry.

**Where the SLAM could run (owner's question, 2026-11-12: "could the SLAM be outsourced to the controller?"):**
- **A close precedent (SRC: the NanoSLAM paper, ETH Zurich, arXiv 2309.12008, and its repository):** graph-based SLAM running fully on board a 44 g nano-drone with four VL53L5CX time-of-flight sensors, on a GAP9 (a RISC-V parallel ultra-low-power processor, under 180 mW), 4.5 cm mapping accuracy, under 250 ms end to end, 87.9 mW budget. The authors say earlier nano-robot SLAM work offloaded the computation to an external base station, and that fully onboard SLAM needed this class of processor. The map memory figures were not found.
- **On the rover's PIC32MX250 (single core, 40 to 50 MHz, 32 KB of RAM; I believe no floating-point unit, not in what I read):** a full SLAM with pose correction is not realistic (my reading; the precedent needed a parallel processor and 250 ms per update, and in a cyclic loop that would starve the motors; Sojourner could not even drive and run its radio at once). A **local occupancy grid with a known pose** (no pose correction) is cheap: a 64 x 64 grid is 4 KB at one byte per cell and a range frame touches about a thousand cells (my estimate), so the PIC32 could keep a small hazard map for reflexes.
- **On the operator's PC (the base-station route, and what Sojourner's ground software did):** realistic and simple; the rover sends ranges, strut angles, wheel counts and inertial data, the PC builds and corrects the 3D model. This is my suggestion (see the variant above). If the PC maps, the OpenMV's advantage over a simple JPEG camera (Arducam, a serial JPEG camera) shrinks to its MicroPython and optional vision, because the rover then only needs to capture stills on request.

**What counted as a hazard for Sojourner (owner's question, 2026-11-12; SRC: Morrison and Nguyen, "On-Board Software for the Mars Pathfinder Microrover", JPL, read from an archived copy; the rover description page; a 1997 JPL feature on its "smarts"):**
- **Method:** the rover drove forward in short segments and stopped between them (motors off) to scan. The optical proximity scanner (infrared lasers and CCD imagers, five laser diodes projecting stripes) built a sparse, approximate map of terrain height ahead. Operators normally gave it a waypoint path from the lander's 3D stereo images, so it did not need to be "a robust maze-solver"; it only had to deal with unexpected problems.
- **Proximity hazards, three conditions searched in that map:** (1) missing data inside the minimum required detection range, taken as a possible drop-off; (2) a height difference between any two adjacent detections above a risk-level-dependent threshold, taken as a rock or a hole; (3) a height difference between the lowest and the highest detections above a higher threshold, taken as a steep slope. Response: turn in place about its centre to avoid it (towards the smaller avoidance turn, or the goal; the same direction on re-scans so it does not bounce between two hazards), then drive straight a few segments before arcing back to the goal; an option to "squeeze" through narrow gaps for a limited distance and back out if stuck.
- **Non-proximity hazards, monitored while moving (inclination and motor currents sampled):** a sensor reading outside safe limits (a table keyed to a risk-level parameter); being too close to the lander and heading towards it; physical contact sensing; the operator's timeout. Response: abort the traverse, unless the operator allowed the rover to handle it, when it backs up, turns and tries again.
- **Device hazards:** every powered device's current is monitored, and a current above its limit marks the device failed and "keep off" (protecting the batteries and electronics from a short circuit); devices and sensors carry a health status that goes down on out-of-range readings and recovers if they behave. The paper's stated aim: time-critical anomalies, "such as power drains and obstacle contact", are handled without the operator.
- **Numbers:** **the paper does not give the thresholds** (they are risk-level tables). Elsewhere: the wheel diameter is 13 cm and the rover could cross obstacles about a wheel diameter in size; a JPL feature says it could tell a two- or three-inch rock it could drive over from a ten- or twelve-inch one that might tip it; higher risk levels let it drive over larger obstacles and the highest let it climb rocks nearly as tall as its wheels; it could plan only about 20 cm ahead; top speed 0.4 m/min; position by dead reckoning from inertial sensors and wheel encoders. The wheel encoders and the inertial sensors are what this rover still lacks.
- **What it suggests for this rover (mine):** the same three proximity conditions can be read from a multizone ranging sensor (a missing return is a drop-off, a jump between neighbouring zones is a rock or hole, the spread across the field is a slope), the non-proximity ones from an inertial sensor, the servo and motor loads, and contact; the operator-set risk level and the stop-and-scan rhythm suit a cyclic loop. Not designed.

**Design note: is it fun? (owner's question, 2026-11-12: Sojourner worked that way because of a light-time delay and no repair; this rover has neither, so the scheme is a game, and the question is whether the game is fun.)**
- **The constraints are different things (mine):** (a) *command delay*, which this rover does not have and need not impose: vehicle-teleoperation work reports that at about 200 to 300 ms of delay operators drop continuous steering for the slower, more error-prone "move and wait" (Ferrell and Sheridan's experiments found it works but takes longer and is less smooth); (b) *low-fidelity perception* (the 3D model, stills that take tens of seconds), a chosen fog of war; (c) *autonomy* (reflexes that avoid hazards by themselves), which takes agency from the operator. The adopted scheme (live data to a model, stills on request) imposes (b), and (c) is still open.
- **What others found (SRC, by search):** the game *Duskers* is built on this premise (drones piloted from a command line through sensors and cameras; reviewers praise the tension of limited information, motion sensors that say something is there but not what, equipment that degrades, and call it punishing but a pleasure to master, with the interface the choice most likely to put players off at first). JPL rover planners work in pairs, plan a whole day's sequence at a time, and one compared it to "the most complicated video game in the world"; they like watching new images downlink; "by the time you see a cliff coming, you've already gone over it". Game-design writing: fog of war should be used only if it increases strategic options; hidden information that can only be uncovered by costly trial and error turns strategy into repetition; choices need a real risk of a bad outcome; friction should be structured, not removed.
- **My view:** the stop, scan and wait ritual on its own is likely to be boring; it is fun when control is immediate and the decisions have stakes. The rover already has natural stakes: a battery and solar charging (dim light makes the lamp and the stills cost energy), the risk of tipping and the self-righting that follows, table edges and steps, and the real room. Levers: a scarce, valuable photo (time, lamp power, radio), the map building up as the rover explores, an operator-set risk level (Sojourner had one), reflexes as a safety net rather than the driver. **The drive speed matters more than the control scheme:** the design speed is about 40 mm/s, 3 m in 75 s.
- **Keep the options open (mine):** if the rover only sends sensor data and the autonomy is firmware, the game can be changed in software: a "pilot" mode (live driving through the model, safety stop only), a "Sojourner" mode (waypoints, onboard hazard avoidance, stills; deliberately authentic and hard), perhaps an artificial delay. The hardware (sensors, a link with low latency, a camera for stills) serves all of them. **A cheap test before any parts are bought:** build the console against a simulated rover (a simple room with noisy ranges and pose) and play the modes for a while; or play *Duskers* as a reference.

## Open decisions (owner)

Settled 2026-11-12: the operator's console does the SLAM and shows a 3D model; the rover sends sensor data and, on request, a high-resolution still at roughly SSTV speed; no continuous video; the headlamp is for stills; one modest serial radio replaces the Wi-Fi link (a 900 MHz module of the XBee-PRO 900HP kind, FCC approved, is the example found); the PIC32 as the single controller; no Linux board.

1. **Onboard reflexes:** whether the rover also avoids hazards by itself (a small local hazard map and simple reflexes, as Sojourner did) or is driven by the operator through the model alone.
